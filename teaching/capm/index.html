Fit the line

Each dot is one . Left to right is the market's excess return that ; bottom to top is 's. Try to draw the line that best follows the cloud. Dashed segments are your line's misses.

Excel's =SLOPE(stock excess, market excess) and =INTERCEPT(…) pick the one line with the smallest total squared miss. That is all a regression is.

Rstock = α + β × Rmarket + ε

Where β and α come from

The best-fitting line has an exact formula, so nobody really has to search for it. Write Ri for the stock's excess return and Rm for the market's, in each of the n s, and put a bar on top for an average. Then:

β=Cov(Ri,Rm)Var(Rm)=∑t=1n(Rm,t−R‾m)(Ri,t−R‾i)∑t=1n(Rm,t−R‾m)2

β is the covariance of the stock with the market, divided by the variance of the market. In words: in the s when the market was above its average, how far above its own average was the stock, relative to how far the market moved? Once β is known, the line must pass through the point of averages, which gives α:

α=R‾i−β×R‾m

And R², the share of the stock's variance explained by the market:

R2=β2×Var(Rm)Var(Ri)

β, the slope, says how much the stock moves with the market. α, the intercept, is where the line crosses the vertical axis: the stock's average excess return in a when the market did exactly zero. ε, the miss, is each dot's distance from the line: the firm-specific part of that , such as earnings surprises, product news, or lawsuits.

How sure are we about β? A different stretch of history would give a somewhat different line. The reasonable range above is wide for volatile stocks and short windows.

6

Two kinds of risk

What we are doing: using the line from Step 4 to split the stock's risk into two pieces. Risk here means variance, the average squared distance of returns from their mean. Because every return is market part + α + miss, and the misses are unrelated to the market, the variances add up:

Var(Ri)total risk=β2×Var(Rm)market risk+Var(ε)firm-specific risk

Step by step, for (all numbers per ):

    Now diversify. Suppose you split your money equally across N stocks like this one: same β, but each with its own unrelated firm news. Every stock feels the same market, so the market part stays. The firm-specific parts partly cancel: good news at one firm offsets bad news at another, and their variance shrinks by a factor of N:

    Var(Rp)=β2×Var(Rm)+Var(ε)N

    Why this matters for the CAPM. Firm-specific risk is easy to get rid of, so investors are not paid for bearing it. Market risk cannot be diversified away, so that is the risk that has to earn a reward. β measures how much market risk a stock adds, which is why β is the only input in the CAPM.

    7

    Is α skill or luck?

    The computer invents a fake stock with the same β and the same amount of firm-specific noise, but a true α of exactly zero. It lives through the same market history, and we run the same regression. Any α it shows is pure luck. Repeat many times.

    Reading it. A stock with a lot of firm-specific noise can show a large α over a few years by luck alone. And with thousands of stocks out there, some will land far out in the tail by chance. Looking back and picking the winner is easy. Knowing in advance which stock will have a positive α is not. That is why the CAPM treats the expected α as zero.

    Part III · From β to the CAPM

    9

    Many stocks, one line

    One stock only gives us a β. The CAPM makes a claim about all stocks together: average returns should rise in a straight line with β. The line starts at zero for β = 0 (T-bills) and passes through the market at β = 1. This is the security market line.

    E[Ri] = βi × E[Rmarket] and αi = 0 for every stock

    To test it, researchers sort all U.S. stocks every year by β into ten groups. Each dot is one group, measured from July 1963 to August 2026.

    See the numbers

    What the picture says. Higher β does come with somewhat higher average returns, which is the core CAPM idea. But the real line is flatter than the CAPM line: low-β stocks did better than predicted, and high-β stocks did worse. It is one of the best-known findings in finance, and part of why later models add factors like SMB and HML.

    Gather the three CAPM inputs

    What we are doing: getting ready to compute the return investors should require from , per year, going forward. The CAPM formula needs three inputs:

    E[ri]=rftoday+βifrom the past×(E[rm]−rf)market risk premium

    β comes from the past because we cannot observe the future co-movement, so we use history as our best guess. It has no units, so a β estimated from ly data can be used directly with annual rates. The other two inputs describe today, because we want the return investors require from now on.

    Input 1: the risk-free rate today

    Take the current yield on a U.S. Treasury, as an annual rate. Nothing to convert: FRED already quotes it per year, and the answer we want is per year.

      The required return

      All assets under the same choices, from lowest to highest required return:

      Look at the ranking. Low-β assets such as Coca-Cola, utilities and gold move little with the market, so investors ask for little more than the risk-free rate. High-β assets such as Nvidia and the leveraged funds amplify market swings, so they need a much higher expected return to be worth holding. Negative-β assets, such as the short S&P 500 fund and the volatility fund, need less than the risk-free rate. They pay off when everything else falls, so they work like insurance, and investors accept a low or even negative expected return for that protection. How risky an asset is on its own plays no role. Only β does.

      The recipe on one card

      1. Download month-end Adj Close for the stock and SPY from Yahoo Finance, last 5 years.
      2. Returns: =B3/B2−1, filled down.
      3. Risk-free: FRED TB3MS ÷ 100 ÷ 12. Subtract it from both returns.
      4. β: =SLOPE(stock excess, market excess). Check its range and try other windows.
      5. Pick today's rf and a market risk premium (often 4–6%).
      6. Required return = rf + β × premium.

      What to remember

      1. β is a slope. It measures how strongly a stock moves with the market, and it is the only risk the CAPM rewards.
      2. α is what is left over. The CAPM says it should be zero on average. Past α, even a big one, is noisy and says little about the future.
      3. Firm-specific risk washes out in a diversified portfolio. Market risk does not.
      4. The CAPM is a statement about all stocks at once: average returns should line up with β. In the data they do, but along a flatter line than the theory predicts.
